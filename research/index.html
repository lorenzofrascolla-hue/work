The Silicon-Power Gap: supporting files

The paper argues that electrical transformer supply and grid connections, not AI chips, limit how fast AI data centers can be built.

These are the supporting files for version 39 of the paper (6 August 2026). The paper itself is deliberately not posted on this site; this page holds the material behind it: the sources, the corrections, what senior readers said, how AI was used, and the formulas. It is independent student research.

On this page: source list, correction log, what the senior readers said, how AI was used, the formulas and where I learned them, related writing, Excel model.

The option model

The option was written on two different things Fixed in v38

What was wrong: The value being bought (S, $61M) was the benefit of speeding up one 75 MW build, but the price paid (K, $42M) was the full remaining cost of the build. Those two cannot be paired in one option.

What changed: Both now sit on the speed-up. S is its discounted present value ($55.1M). K is the carrying cost of pulling a $450M fit-out forward by two years ($37.9M).

The price paid was too low by about ten times Fixed in v38

What was wrong: $42M for 75 MW implies $0.56M per MW. Published construction cost indices put the comparable cost near $6.0M per MW. The low figure was the main reason the option looked so valuable.

What changed: The $6.0M per MW benchmark is stated and cited.

An undiscounted sum was called a present value Fixed in v38

What was wrong: $0.40M per MW per year times 75 MW times two years was described as a present value without being discounted.

What changed: The arithmetic is shown: $30M received at 1.5 and 2.5 years, discounted at 4.4%, gives $55.1M.

A growth rate was labelled as a volatility Fixed in v38

What was wrong: A table column headed "Implied σ" held compound price growth rates, not a measure of spread. It read as support for the 35% volatility input.

What changed: The column is relabelled, with a note that a steadily rising cost curve is evidence of drift, not of volatility.

A $24M "moat premium" had no derivation Fixed in v38

What was wrong: An extra $24M was added for correlated supply constraints, on top of an option value that already prices scarcity. Nothing connected the correlation to that dollar figure.

What changed: The premium is withdrawn. The correlation finding stays as a description only.

Transformer physics and engineering

The transformer aging section used the wrong standard Fixed in v38

What was wrong: The section used a 20 °C reference temperature and attributed it to IEEE. IEEE C57.12.00 rates transformers at 30 °C; the 20 °C convention belongs to IEC 60076. The claim that Miami ages 38% faster than the reference pointed the wrong way.

What changed: The section is rebuilt on the correct basis, as a relative index against Northern Virginia.

Heat pass-through was understated Fixed in v38

What was wrong: A 0.594 factor was applied between outside temperature and the transformer hot-spot temperature.

What changed: At constant load the pass-through is one to one, and the figures are rerun on that basis.

A hand-typed sign error in the Dallas row Fixed in v38

What was wrong: Every row followed the same rule except Dallas, where a cooler site produced a hotter result.

What changed: Corrected.

Insulation terminology was mixed up Fixed in v38

What was wrong: "Class A insulation, 110 °C reference" joined two different things, and the governing equation was attributed to Annex G of IEEE C57.91 when it is in Clause 5.2.

What changed: Corrected.

Aging was calculated at the annual average temperature Fixed in v38

What was wrong: The aging factor rises faster than temperature does, so using one annual average understates it, most of all in places with large seasonal swings.

What changed: The factor is now added up month by month from climate normals. The Miami to Helsinki aging ratio is 6.0x (it was 3.3x in v37).

Service lives that no utility observes Fixed in v38

What was wrong: Heat aging alone implied a 32 year life in Miami and 192 years in Helsinki.

What changed: Replaced by expected life that also counts non-heat failures: 18.6 years in Miami, 29.6 in Northern Virginia, 36.5 in Helsinki, 38.7 in Luleå.

A saving was booked for a pump that does not exist Fixed in v38

What was wrong: The paper named a cooling class with no oil pump (ONAN/ONAF), then calculated pumping work and booked a $41K per year saving from it.

What changed: The line is deleted. It was worth $6M of the Operator H figure.

Transformer losses were six times too high Fixed in v38

What was wrong: Losses were taken as 2.0% of throughput. Published typical figures for a 100 MVA unit are about 0.40%.

What changed: 0.35% is used and every flow figure is rerun. No downstream conclusion moves.

The ester fluid conclusion had the wrong sign Fixed in v38

What was wrong: The paper said natural ester fluid costs 11% less to circulate. It is about three times as thick as mineral oil at operating temperature, so it costs more.

What changed: Corrected. The real case for ester, longer insulation life, is added.

A saving was counted twice Fixed in v38

What was wrong: The transformer cooling saving and the facility efficiency (PUE) saving were added together. PUE is measured at the utility meter and already includes transformer losses.

What changed: The claim is withdrawn.

The free-cooling rule left out two temperature gaps Fixed in v38

What was wrong: The rule omitted the cooling-tower gap (5 to 6 K) and the heat-exchanger gap (2 to 3 K).

What changed: The net threshold is stated. The more conservative threshold is kept, so the Nordic advantage shown is the smaller of two readings.

What the senior readers said

Four senior practitioners gave written critiques of the paper in June 2026 (versions 32 and 33), and one professor corresponded with me about the repricing argument in July 2026. Each is named here with their permission. This is the substance of what each said, in my words, and what changed in the paper because of it.

Riccardo Bertocco

Wharton MBA; former partner at Bain; former managing director and partner at BCG leading its power and utilities work

What they said: The 35% volatility input, borrowed from the spot price of H100 GPUs, is not a defensible stand-in for the volatility of a transformer slot, and that one number drives the option value. The hardest technical push in the whole review.

What changed: Version 33 extended the sensitivity grid down to 15% and 20% volatility and conceded in the appendix that the GPU proxy is the wrong object. In August I found on my own that the option itself was mis-specified and cut the per-slot value from $21.8M to $19.7M (version 38), which makes his point weigh more, not less.

Gonzalo Binello

Head of Latin America, Schroders

What they said: Small modular reactors and on-site generation are the escape hatch: if data centers generate their own power they bypass the grid queue and the transformer makers the thesis rests on. He called it the cleanest break in the thesis’s timing.

What changed: Version 33 strengthened the section on behind-the-meter generation with 2026 turbine-backlog and nuclear-commitment data, arguing that on-site generation moves the chokepoint to turbines and switchgear rather than removing it, and conceded that the Nordic leg weakens if reactor timelines compress. In August I found and corrected four factual errors in that same section (version 38); one of them cuts his way.

Mariano Scandizzo

Head of Asset Management, Insigneo; CFA, CQF

What they said: Compute that can be scheduled, shifted in time or moved between sites could dissolve a site-level transformer constraint before it ever binds, so the grid-connection wait may be softer than the paper claims.

What changed: I first recorded his point as already answered by the existing text, which was wrong. A dedicated section on demand-side flexibility and schedulable load was then written, engaging Norris et al. (2025) and Knittel, Senga and Wang (2025), and the paper now states demand-side flexibility as an open research frontier.

Alejo Czerwonko

Chief Investment Officer, Emerging Markets Americas, UBS; adjunct associate professor, Columbia SIPA

What they said: Pressed the framing and the inputs through an emerging-markets and macro lens. A supportive read; no single input was challenged to destruction.

What changed: The framing was tightened where he pressed (version 33). No structural change.

Thomas Lyon

Dow Chair of Sustainable Science, Technology and Commerce, Michigan Ross

What they said: In July 2026 he explained that his audit-threat framework (Lyon and Maxwell, 2011) does not predict any particular route by which such information reaches a market: releasing data to investors may not produce the hoped-for repricing, and a rival might instead need to press the FTC or bring litigation. The paper had assumed one route where several exist.

What changed: Version 39 rewrote the repricing section. It now borrows only the audit-threat framing from his model, not its results; lays out four channels (investor disclosure, EU mandate, FTC enforcement, private litigation) with their decision-makers and timelines; states plainly that the paper cannot tell which channel a rival will choose; and splits the confidence rating into Medium on the mechanism and Low on the timing.

Corrections I found on my own after these reviews are in the correction log above; none of the readers is responsible for them.

The formulas, where they come from, and how I learned them

The paper uses the methods below. None of them is original; the work was learning each one well enough to apply it to transformers and to check the result. Most came from MIT OpenCourseWare courses I worked through on my own: Finance Theory I (Andrew Lo), Analytics of Finance (Leonid Kogan), Thermodynamics and Kinetics, Heat and Mass Transfer, Introduction to Electric Power Systems (James Kirtley), Linear Algebra (Gilbert Strang) and Computational Thinking and Data Science (John Guttag). The finance-modelling conventions I taught myself from public materials: Aswath Damodaran’s open valuation course, the CFI and Wall Street Prep modelling programs, the CFA curriculum and Robert Shiller’s Financial Markets (Yale, via Coursera). The underlying physics I learned mainly from YouTube: Practical Engineering, Real Engineering, 3Blue1Brown and StatQuest among others. All of these are listed in the source list above.

Show the 22 formulas and where I learned them

Black-Scholes-Merton call option

Values the right, but not the obligation, to build on a secured transformer slot ahead of everyone else.

Formula: C = S·N(d₁) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d₂)

Used for: Section I.A, the per-slot option value. Source cited: Black and Scholes (1973); Merton (1973); Hull (2021).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 15.401 Finance Theory I (Lo) and 15.450 Analytics of Finance (Kogan).

Intrinsic value and time value

Splits an option’s value into what it is worth if exercised today and what the remaining wait is worth.

Formula: time value = C − max(S − K, 0)

Used for: Section I.A. Source cited: Hull (2021).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 15.401 Finance Theory I.

Present value and discounting

Turns money arriving in the future into what it is worth today.

Formula: PV = CF / (1 + r)^t; S = Σ CF_t·e^(−rt)

Used for: Section I.A, building the underlying S and the strike K. Source cited: Brealey, Myers and Allen (2019).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 15.401 Finance Theory I.

Annuity factor and net present value

Adds up a stream of equal yearly cash flows at a discount rate.

Formula: PV = A·[1 − (1 + r)^(−n)] / r

Used for: Sections I.B and I.D, the 20-year bridges. Source cited: Brealey, Myers and Allen (2019).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 15.401 Finance Theory I; Wall Street Prep and CFI modelling courses.

Cost of capital (CAPM and WACC)

Sets the discount rate from the risk-free rate, the market premium and the company’s risk.

Formula: E(R) = r_f + β·(E(R_m) − r_f)

Used for: Discount-rate choices throughout Section I. Source cited: Damodaran (2012, 2024).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 15.401 Finance Theory I; Damodaran’s open valuation course.

Compound annual growth rate

The steady yearly rate that produces an observed total change.

Formula: CAGR = (end / start)^(1/years) − 1

Used for: Table A2, transformer price growth. Source cited: Wood Mackenzie (2024).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 15.401; also IB Mathematics.

Arrhenius aging factor

How much faster transformer insulation ages at a hotter spot temperature.

Formula: F_AA = exp(15000/383 − 15000/(θ_H + 273))

Used for: Section I.B, siting and insulation life. Source cited: Arrhenius (1889); IEEE Std C57.91-2011, Clause 5.2.

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 5.60 Thermodynamics and Kinetics.

Hot-spot temperature model

The hottest point in the transformer is the ambient temperature plus the oil rise plus the winding gradient.

Formula: θ_H = θ_A + Δθ_TO + Δθ_H

Used for: Section I.B. Source cited: IEEE Std C57.91-2011; IEEE Std C57.12.00.

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 2.51 Heat and Mass Transfer; 6.061 Electric Power Systems.

Monthly-integrated aging and relative index

Because aging accelerates faster than temperature rises, the factor is added up month by month, then expressed relative to Northern Virginia.

Formula: F_EQA = Σ F_AA(θ_m)·h_m / Σ h_m; index = F_EQA,site / F_EQA,NoVA

Used for: Section I.B, the 6.0x Miami-to-Helsinki spread. Source cited: IEEE Std C57.91-2011; NOAA, FMI and SMHI climate normals.

Where I learned it: Self-taught: the convexity argument from Jensen (1906) and Wackerly, Mendenhall and Scheaffer (2008).

Competing-risks expected life (Weibull)

Combines the heat-driven life with a 45-year non-heat life to get an expected service life a utility would recognise.

Formula: S(t) = S_thermal(t)·S_other(t), S_other = exp(−(t/45)^k)

Used for: Section I.B, expected lives of 18.6 to 38.7 years. Source cited: CIGRE (2015) reliability survey.

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare, with Wackerly, Mendenhall and Scheaffer (2008).

Transformer loss budget

No-load and load losses as a share of throughput, for the cooling and efficiency figures.

Formula: P_loss = P_core + P_copper ≈ 0.35% of rating

Used for: Section I.B. Source cited: Heathcote (2007); Hitachi Energy (2025).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 6.061 Introduction to Electric Power Systems.

Steady-state heat balance

The coolant flow needed to carry away the transformer’s heat.

Formula: Q = ṁ·c_p·ΔT

Used for: Section I.B, cooling fluids. Source cited: Lienhard and Lienhard (2019).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 2.51 Heat and Mass Transfer.

Pump work and viscosity

Circulating a thicker fluid costs more work, which reversed the ester-fluid conclusion in version 38.

Formula: W ∝ Q·Δp, with Δp rising with viscosity

Used for: Section I.B. Source cited: Karassik et al. (2013); Stepanoff (1957).

Where I learned it: YouTube: Practical Engineering (Grady Hillhouse).

Steinmetz core-loss equation

Energy lost in the transformer’s steel core, which is why grain-oriented electrical steel is scarce and valuable.

Formula: P_core = k·f^a·B^b

Used for: Section I.C, electrical steel. Source cited: Steinmetz (1892); Bozorth (1993); Fitzgerald, Kingsley and Umans (2014).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 6.061 Introduction to Electric Power Systems.

Correlation matrix

Measures how much the supply constraints (queues, lead times, steel) move together.

Formula: ρ_xy = cov(x, y) / (σ_x·σ_y)

Used for: Appendix B, constraint co-movement. Source cited: Appendix B workbook.

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 6.0002 Computational Thinking and Data Science.

Principal component analysis

Finds the single shared factor behind the correlated constraints.

Formula: eigenvectors of the correlation matrix

Used for: Appendix B. Source cited: Jolliffe (2002); Strang (2016).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 18.06 Linear Algebra (Strang); 3Blue1Brown for intuition.

Monte Carlo simulation

Draws thousands of possible energisation delays to see how correlation lengthens the tail.

Formula: sample, repeat, read the 95th percentile

Used for: Appendix B, the four-month tail. Source cited: Glasserman (2003).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 6.0002 Computational Thinking and Data Science.

Stefan-Boltzmann radiation

How much heat an orbital radiator can reject, for the off-Earth compute section.

Formula: P = ε·σ·A·T⁴

Used for: Section I.E, orbital data centers. Source cited: Lienhard and Lienhard (2019); IEEE Spectrum (2026).

Where I learned it: MIT OpenCourseWare: 2.51; YouTube: Real Engineering.

Kilowatt-hour to revenue bridge

Turns delivered power into compute hours and revenue, with PUE as the divisor.

Formula: revenue = MW·hours·(1/PUE)·utilisation·price

Used for: Section I.A and the Excel model. Source cited: The Green Grid (2012); NVIDIA (2024).

Where I learned it: Self-taught: CFI and Wall Street Prep modelling courses.

Replacement reserve and insurance rate

A yearly set-aside for replacing a transformer at the end of its expected life, and a premium as a share of insured value.

Formula: reserve = cost / expected life; premium = rate × value

Used for: Sections I.B and I.D. Source cited: IEEE Std C57.12.00; UNEP underwriting module; S&P Global (2026).

Where I learned it: Self-taught: CFI and Wall Street Prep; underwriting guides.

Relative valuation multiples

Compares a company’s price to its earnings, cash flow or funds from operations against peers.

Formula: P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/FFO

Used for: Section II, the company-by-company read. Source cited: Damodaran (2012); CFA Institute (2024).

Where I learned it: Self-taught: the CFA Level I and II equity curriculum; Damodaran’s open course.

Audit-threat disclosure framing

Why a rival with the means to verify a claim can force the market to reprice it.

Formula: Lyon and Maxwell (2011), framing only

Used for: Section I.D.6, the repricing trigger. Source cited: Lyon and Maxwell (2011, 2004).

Where I learned it: Self-taught from the articles; corrected after Professor Lyon’s feedback.
