Paper-trading research system: demo

This is a read-only demo of a research system I built. It is made from saved copies of the system’s own records. It is not connected to the live system, and nothing on this page updates.

Every order described here is simulated. No real money is involved anywhere in the system.

Step 1 of 5

Step 1 of 5What the system does

The system tests one idea from my research: that the companies supplying power and electrical equipment to AI data centers are worth owning.

It runs on my own computer. It reads prices and company filings, scores a list of companies tied to that idea, and checks candidates against 13 risk gates. Every weekday morning an automated run plans simulated orders for a practice account at a broker, and safety checks can cancel those orders before they are sent.

This kind of practice is called paper trading. The account holds a made-up balance, the prices are real, and every buy and sell is simulated.

The next four steps show the gates, what the practice account holds, how its value has moved next to the stock market, and a test of the system that it failed.

Step 2 of 5The 13 risk gates

A gate is a yes or no rule. The system flags a company as a buy candidate only if it passes all 13. If the data for a gate is missing, that gate counts as failed.

Trend

  1. Price is above its 50-day average
  2. Price is above its 200-day average
  3. The 20-day average is above the 50-day average

Momentum

  1. The price is higher than it was 20 trading days ago
  2. The price is not stretched: less than 15% above its 50-day average

Signal

  1. The forecasting model gives better than 62% odds of a higher price
  2. No sudden jump in volatility

Portfolio fit

  1. The portfolio does not already hold it
  2. Enough cash is left over after the buy
  3. Its sector stays under 40% of the portfolio

Financial health

  1. Altman Z-score of 1.81 or higher (not in the distress zone)
  2. Beneish M-score of -1.78 or lower (no sign of manipulated earnings)

Valuation

  1. Price is no more than 25% above the estimate of fair value

An earlier account. A first, smaller practice account ran from 2 June 2026 to 2 July 2026. It went from $100,000 to $91,553, down 8.4%, while the S&P 500 fell 1.7%. On 3 July 2026 the practice account was reset to a simulated $1,000,000. The chart shows the account since that reset.

How these numbers were checked

The S&P 500 line does not use the system’s own numbers. It uses official closing values, starting from the close on 2 July 2026, the last close before the reset (the market was closed on 3 July).

The account line is the value the system recorded for each trading day. Two checks were run on it. First, the benchmark prices the system recorded track the official S&P 500 closes: the ratio between the two stays within 0.3% over the whole period. Second, rebuilding the latest account value from the official closing prices of the 14 stocks lands within 0.3% of what the system recorded. The system’s two separate records of account value agree within 0.3% on 41 of the 42 days they share, and within 0.9% on the other.

On 22 days the system’s history file held a row from a different, built-in sample portfolio instead of the practice account. Those rows are left out. 4 trading days have no usable record, and the line runs straight across them.

Step 5 of 5The test it failed

Verdict: not established. The ranking failed 3 of 5 criteria.

The part of the system that ranks companies did not earn its place.

What was tested

The system ranks companies and puts more of the simulated money into the ones it ranks highest. On 1 and 2 August 2026 that ranking was put through a walk-forward test: the rules are set using one stretch of history, then judged on a later stretch they have never seen.

The test used daily prices from 2016 to 2026 for 66 US-listed companies: the 26 on the research list and 40 unrelated large companies as a control group.

Pre-registered

The five pass or fail criteria were written down and saved before the test ran. All five had to pass. Failing any one meant the verdict would be "not established".

The five criteria and how the ranking did
CriterionResultOutcome
1. Works in most periods: positive in at least 80% of the six-month windows in the development period36% (4/11)Failed
2. Holds up on later data: positive on the 2023 to 2026 test period and better than the simple baseline ruleIR 1.411 vs baseline 1.354Passed
3. Works outside my thesis: positive on a control group of 40 unrelated large companiesIR -0.138, alpha -1.20%Failed
4. Survives the multiple-testing check: the result is large enough given how many variations were triedIR 0.772 vs threshold 0.953Failed
5. Survives a resampling check: block bootstrap p-value below 0.05p = 0.0030Passed

IR is the information ratio, a measure of return beyond the benchmark for the risk taken. One change to the plan is disclosed in the test record: the start of the first period moved from 2016 to April 2017, because no company had the required 250 days of price history before then. It was made after the first run, before any result was chosen, and applied to every candidate.

What it means

Simply holding every company on the list in equal amounts passed all five criteria. Measured against that, the ranking cost about 8 percentage points a year from 2017 to 2022 (−8.07% a year). The picking was a cost, not a benefit.

The test has limits. Ten years of prices for 66 US-listed companies can show that a result was not manufactured by the search for it. It cannot promise anything about the future.

What I did about it

I drafted a proposal to drop the ranking and keep the gates: hold every eligible company in equal amounts, and leave the safety checks in place.

It is a proposal, not a finished change. The ranking code is still in the system. Since 31 August 2026 the automated run has had the equal-amounts approach switched on in the practice account, behind a setting that can be turned off. That is why its notes in step 3 mention a "basket".

Glossary

Paper trading
Practising with simulated orders and a made-up balance. No real money moves.
Practice account
The simulated account at a broker that receives the paper-trades.
Account value (equity)
What the practice account would be worth if everything in it were sold at current prices, including cash.
S&P 500
An index that tracks 500 large US companies. It is the usual yardstick for the US stock market.
Benchmark
The yardstick a portfolio is compared with. Here it is the S&P 500.
Risk gate
A yes or no rule a company must pass before the system will flag it as a buy candidate.
Exposure limit
A cap on the total size of the portfolio compared with the account value.
Ranking
Ordering companies by score so that more money goes to the highest-scored ones.
Equal amounts (equal weight)
Putting the same amount into every company instead of favouring some.
Walk-forward test
A test where rules are set on earlier data and then judged on later data they have not seen.
Pre-registered
The pass or fail rules were written down before the test ran, so they could not be adjusted to fit the result.
Control group
Unrelated companies used to check whether a method works in general or only on the companies it was built around.
Altman Z-score, Beneish M-score
Two standard accounting checks. The first flags companies at risk of financial distress. The second flags signs that earnings may have been manipulated.
